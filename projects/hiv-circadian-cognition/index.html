Circadian rhythms, sleep, and cognitive health in people living with HIV

 Peng Li, Ph.D.
 1 words
 1 min read

This page is intended to showcase what we have found from two related pilot projects: “Association between rest activity circadian rhythm and cognition in PLWH” (supported by the HIV & Aging Research Consortium) and “Circadian disturbances and cognitive impairment in people living with HIV” (supported by the Harvard University Center for AIDS Research). While our primary audience is the research community, we are trying our best to have plain language summaries for each major finding. We hope that this practice will be of help for the broader community to benefit from our research. Note that this page will be updated as new findings from these projects become available.

With effective antiretroviral therapy, people living with HIV (PLWH) are living longer, but they face higher risks of chronic inflammation, cognitive impairment, and frailty as they age. Sleep and circadian rhythms are closely linked to these outcomes in the general population, yet their health relevance in PLWH remains understudied. Using wearable actigraphy, novel data-adaptive tools for analyzing rest-activity rhythms, and sleep assessments, these projects were designed to determine whether disturbances in daily rest-activity rhythms and sleep are linked to cognitive and broader health outcomes in PLWH.

Delayed rest-activity rhythms are linked to inflammation and slower cognitive processing in PLWH

People living with HIV whose daily activity peaked later in the day had slower reaction times and higher levels of inflammation.

We analyzed data from 87 PLWH in the UK Biobank who had valid wrist actigraphy recordings and no dementia diagnosis. Using uniform phase empirical mode decomposition (UPMEMD), a data-adaptive method we developed for circadian analysis, we extracted the phase of the circadian rest-activity rhythm, a marker of the timing of peak daily activity. We then examined how circadian phase related to cognitive performance (reaction time) and systemic inflammation (C-reactive protein, CRP), adjusting for age and sex. A delayed circadian phase was associated with both slower reaction time and higher CRP: each 1-SD delay in phase corresponded to a 0.18–0.21 SD increase in these outcomes (both p = 0.02). To put this in the context of aging, the effect of a 1-SD delay in circadian phase was comparable to that of approximately 6–7 years of aging. Circadian amplitude was not associated with either outcome.

These findings highlight the potential role of circadian function in cognitive and inflammatory outcomes among PLWH and support further research on interventions targeting circadian alignment in this population.

  • Haghayegh S, Parker RA, Montano MA, Katz IT, Hu K, Li P. Delayed phase in rest-activity rhythms is associated with inflammation and cognitive performance in people living with HIV. SLEEP Advances. 2025;6(3):zpaf055. DOI: 10.1093/sleepadvances/zpaf055

Poor sleep is linked to greater frailty in PLWH in Botswana

In Botswana, a setting with high HIV prevalence, people living with HIV were nearly three times as likely as their peers without HIV to report poor sleep, and those who slept poorly were frailer.

PLWH are at increased risk of frailty, a state of reduced resilience to stressors that raises the risk of adverse health outcomes. In this cross-sectional pilot study, we enrolled 50 middle-aged PLWH on suppressive antiretroviral therapy (age: 56.9 ± 4.4 years; 26 females) and 50 matched HIV-seronegative controls (age: 56.6 ± 4.3 years; 24 females) in Botswana. Sleep quality was rated as “good” or “poor” based on two self-reported sleep questions, and a frailty index was built from 33 health deficits spanning body mass index, waist circumference, physical activity, emotional status, and fatigue (ranging from 0, no deficit present, to 1, all deficits present). PLWH had 2.88-fold higher odds of reporting poor sleep than controls (95% CI: 1.22–6.79, p = 0.02). Poor sleep was associated with a higher frailty index in PLWH but not in controls: PLWH who reported poor sleep had a frailty index 0.15 higher than those who reported good sleep (95% CI: 0.09–0.21, p < 0.0001).

These findings highlight the importance of sleep health in understanding frailty risk in PLWH.

  • Zheng X, Cai R, Gao C, Ponatshego P, Gao L, Montano M, Hu K, Mosepele M, Li P. Self-reported sleep quality and frailty in middle-aged people living with HIV in an African setting: a pilot study. AIDS Care. 2026 Mar;38(3):650–660. DOI: 10.1080/09540121.2025.2564882 PMCID: PMC12826803
